# Indie Game Market Report — November 2026

**Window covered:** 10 October – 10 November 2026
**Scope:** games priced at $20 or less, small teams (under 10 people where known), no AAA/AA.
**Compiled:** 10 November 2026

## How to read this report

- **Sales figures are developer-announced or third-party estimates**, never Valve-verified. Each one is attributed.
- **Revenue figures are my own arithmetic** (units × launch price). They are gross ceilings before Steam's 30% cut, refunds, VAT and regional pricing. Real net to the developer is typically 40–55% of that number, and less again with a publisher.
- Where a section says **"inference"**, it is my reading of the evidence, not something a source reported.
- Where marketing channels could not be found in any source, the report says so instead of guessing.

## The month in one paragraph

Three of the seven games below are co-op "friendslop" at $5–$10, and all three sold on being funny to watch with friends. The two $20 games both won on a tightly defined niche plus a long demo/playtest runway. October's Steam Next Fest had 7,319 demos (2.5x a year earlier) and the median game gained **60 wishlists** from it, so the fest now rewards games that arrive with an audience and does very little for games that arrive without one. Generative-AI accusations appeared in the reviews of four of the seven games.

## 1. Probably Stolen – Cyberpunk Shopkeeper Sim

**Genre:** pawn-shop management sim / RPG, single-player, pixel art
**Platform:** PC (Steam), Steam Deck Playable
**Team:** Questing Goose Studio, a small Montreal studio (exact headcount not published); published by tinyBuild
**Price:** $19.99, 10% launch discount
**Sales:** 350k in 24 hours and 500k in two days (developer/publisher); ~750k by 3 November (GameDiscoverCo estimate, not confirmed by the publisher). Peak concurrent players reported between 152k and 174k depending on the snapshot.
**Gross ceiling:** roughly $13–15M.
**Reviews:** ~96% positive on 7,000+ reviews.

### Why it succeeded

- **The same game failed first.** It was originally released as a demo called *Deepspace Emporium*, which peaked at 28 concurrent players. tinyBuild's CEO said they "loved the concept and gameplay" and worked with the developer to redo the visuals and narrow the design to the parts that worked. The mechanics were already good; the packaging was what nobody clicked on.
- **The name states the genre.** "Cyberpunk Shopkeeper Sim" is in the title. A player knows what they are buying from the capsule alone.
- **A fantasy with built-in tension.** You flip goods for rent money, spot fakes, and decide whether to deal in weapons and drugs for higher margins while dodging police inspections. Reviewers describe a "just one more day" loop.
- **A long, large funnel.** Open Steam playtest from 3 August, demo from 14 September, then Next Fest in late October, then launch two days after the fest ended. The playtest had 246k activations and 180k players, 45k of whom played over 20 hours.
- **China.** 51% of playtest activations were from China, 17% from the US.

### Marketing breakdown

- **Steam Next Fest: yes, and it was the highest-performing demo by concurrent players** (39,787 peak) and #2 by unique players. It gained 160,900 wishlists during the fest and ended at about 638,700.
- **Launch timing was strategic:** released 28 October, two days after the fest closed, while the demo audience was still warm.
- **Publisher-led.** tinyBuild's CEO pushed the "28 players to 100k" comeback story on X after launch, which became the headline for most press coverage.
- No source I found documents specific TikTok, Reddit or streamer activity.
- **Luck or strategy:** strategy. The rework, the staged playtest → demo → fest → launch sequence and the localisation into China were all deliberate.

### What a solo developer can replicate

- If a demo gets no traction but the people who do play it stay for hours, treat it as a positioning problem before treating it as a design problem. Change the name, capsule and art direction and test again.
- Put the genre in the title.
- Run an open Steam playtest months before the demo. It costs nothing, and it builds the review base that makes launch-day reviews land at 96%.
- Launch within days of Next Fest ending instead of months later.
- Localise into Simplified Chinese if your genre is management/sim.

### Red flags

- The first version was functionally dead. Without a publisher willing to fund a full visual rework, this is a 28-player game.
- Players report the mid-game becomes repetitive, plus UI complaints and minor technical issues.
- A solo developer cannot reproduce the scale of this result; the publisher's budget and reach were part of it.

## 2. Moon Sweepers

**Genre:** co-op incremental cleaning/automation game, up to 16 players
**Platform:** PC (Steam, Windows only)
**Team:** DragDrop Games, self-published. One fan site describes a two-person Finnish team; I could not confirm this from an official source.
**Price:** $8.99 ($7.64 at launch)
**Sales:** 600k+ in its first few days (developer); >700k in October (GameDiscoverCo). 80k+ concurrent players on its first weekend. #8 on Steam's weekly top sellers by revenue for 27 Oct – 3 Nov.
**Gross ceiling:** roughly $5M.
**Reviews:** 78% positive on ~4,300 reviews ("Mostly Positive").

### Why it succeeded

- **It merges two proven loops.** You start cleaning the Moon with a toothbrush, sell the dust, buy bigger brooms, then a sweeper truck, then solar panels and conveyor belts. That is the satisfaction of a cleaning sim, the number-go-up of an incremental and the late game of an automation game in one progression curve.
- **16-player lobbies.** Most co-op hits cap at 4–8. A whole Discord server can join one session, which multiplies copies sold per friend group.
- **Impulse price.** Under $8 at launch, so nobody in the group needs convincing.
- **The premise is a one-sentence joke** that works as a clip title.

### Marketing breakdown

- No source I found documents the campaign. Coverage describes it only as "super-viral", with concurrent players climbing through launch weekend, which is the pattern of word of mouth inside friend groups.
- **Inference:** mostly organic, driven by format (large lobbies, low price) more than by a planned campaign.

### What a solo developer can replicate

- Combine two established loops instead of inventing one. Each loop here is simple on its own.
- Raise the lobby cap if your netcode can take it.
- Ship with a simple late game and announce the next update immediately. The developer called the game "nowhere near finished" and dated update 1.1 for December within days of launch.

### Red flags

- **78% positive is weak for a hit this size.** Reviews complain about a thin late game, bugs and performance in large bases.
- **AI backlash.** The store page discloses generative AI only for translation. Some reviews claim far more of the game is AI-generated. That is unverified, but the argument is taking up space in the review section.
- 16-player networking is a serious engineering cost for a solo developer.

## 3. Scam With Your Friends

**Genre:** co-op voice-chat comedy; you run a scam call centre and talk to AI-driven targets
**Platform:** PC (Steam), Early Access
**Team:** two credited developers (JakeHub and Metater), self-published as Jatater Worldwide
**Price:** $9.99
**Sales:** >600k in October (GameDiscoverCo); Gamalytic estimates ~720k with a wide range (440k–1M). 28,983 concurrent players on day one.
**Gross ceiling:** roughly $6M, before the AI running costs below.

### Why it succeeded

- **The game generates clips by design.** Players speak out loud to characters that answer in real time through a language model. Every session produces unscripted, funny audio, which is exactly what streamers and short-form video need.
- **A premise that explains itself in the title**, attached to a format (scam-baiting) that already has a large YouTube audience.
- **Friendslop pricing:** $10, bought in groups.

### Marketing breakdown

- GameDiscoverCo reports "big influencer interest". Fan sites reference a playtest and YouTube/TikTok activity before launch. I could not find named creators or numbers.
- **Inference:** creator-led. The game was built to be watched.

### What a solo developer can replicate

- Design a mechanic whose normal play produces shareable moments, so marketing is a by-product of play.
- Voice input as a core mechanic is still underused.

### Red flags

- **Concurrent players are already well below peak** (GameDiscoverCo).
- **Mixed early reviews** over AI connectivity failures, speech recognition and multiplayer problems at launch.
- **Per-player running costs.** The game depends on paid third-party language-model services (the store page lists OpenRouter and DeepSeek). A one-time $10 purchase funds an ongoing cost, and a Steam discussion thread is already warning buyers about it. A solo developer copying this model takes on a bill that grows with success.

## 4. Queen's Domain

**Genre:** first-person dark-fantasy action RPG in the style of *King's Field*
**Platform:** PC, PS5, Xbox Series X|S, Switch 2
**Team:** Freshly Baked Games, two people; published by Future Friends Games
**Price:** $19.99 ($16.99 at launch)
**Sales:** 100k in about five days (developer, 4 November). Peak 11,577 concurrent players on 8 November, nine days after launch.
**Gross ceiling:** roughly $1.7M on Steam for the first 100k.
**Reviews:** rose from Very Positive to Overwhelmingly Positive (~96%).

### Why it succeeded

- **It serves a starved niche by name.** FromSoftware fans have wanted a new *King's Field* for decades. The developers copied the series' logo typeface, so the audience recognised the pitch before reading a word.
- **A committed look.** Low-resolution textures, hard dithering, PS1-era lighting. The developers call it "crunchy". It is cheap for two people to produce and instantly identifiable in a three-second clip.
- **One distinctive mechanic:** the sword doubles as a traversal tool that unlocks new areas as it upgrades.
- **Concurrent players peaked in week two, not on day one.** That is word of mouth, not a marketing spike.

### Marketing breakdown

- **A showcase reveal did the heavy lifting.** Announced at the PC Gaming Show in December 2025; 50k wishlists in the first week.
- **Wishlist runway of ~11 months:** 50k → 180k+ at the release-date announcement → reportedly 300k+ at launch (the last figure is from a single outlet).
- **Short-form clips with a voice.** Instagram clips with captions like "my teeth would be worn down if i was to chew on my game".
- **A free demo on Steam for months.** Players asked the developers to keep a sword glitch that launches you forward, which is a sign of a community forming around the demo.
- **Press:** repeat coverage from PC Gamer, GamesRadar, 80.lv and Kill Screen.
- **Publisher:** Future Friends Games (also *CloverPit*), which handled the simultaneous console launch.
- **Luck or strategy:** strategy, with patience.

### What a solo developer can replicate

- Pick a dormant series with a loud fanbase and say plainly that you are making its spiritual successor.
- Choose an art style that is both cheap and distinctive.
- Keep a demo live for months and post clips consistently.
- Apply to showcases. One slot produced 50k wishlists in a week.

### Red flags

- PC Gamer ran a piece saying first-person dungeon crawlers are reaching "peak saturation". This game is the exception that stood out; the next one in the genre will have a harder time.
- At $20 and with this scope, it took two people roughly a year from reveal alone, plus whatever came before.
- A four-platform simultaneous launch is not realistic without a publisher.

## 5. Worst Class Ever

**Genre:** asymmetric multiplayer party game, students versus teacher, up to 8 players
**Platform:** PC (Steam)
**Team:** Funcore Games (size not published); published by 2P Games, a China-founded publisher
**Price:** $6.99, 35% off for 14 days ($4.54)
**Sales:** none announced; it launched on 9 November. Peak 35,774 concurrent players on 10 November.
**Reviews:** ~87% positive on a small early sample (about 120 reviews).

### Why it succeeded

- **Most-played demo of October's Steam Next Fest:** about 600k unique players and a 27.8k concurrent peak.
- **A universal premise.** Everyone has been a student misbehaving behind a teacher's back. It needs no explanation in any language.
- **Asymmetric roles create stories.** Students score "mischief" with smoke bombs, drones and ink; the teacher hunts them with cameras and radar.
- **Aggressive price.** $4.54 at launch.

### Marketing breakdown

- **Steam Next Fest was the entire launch platform**, and it was used deliberately: demo pulled on 3 November, paid launch six days later, two weeks after the fest. Demo players who wanted more had to buy.
- Press release to games media announcing the #1 ranking.
- No documented TikTok, Reddit or streamer campaign in the sources I found.
- **Luck or strategy:** strategy in the timing; the size of the demo audience probably owes a lot to the publisher's reach in China (inference).

### What a solo developer can replicate

- Pulling the demo shortly before launch converts demo players into buyers.
- A deep launch discount on an already low price suits games that sell in groups.
- Pick a premise that needs no localisation to understand.

### Red flags

- **Retention.** A SteamDB snapshot showed about 1,600 players live against a 35.8k all-time peak. That may reflect time of day, but it is a steep ratio on launch week.
- 600k free demo players at a $4.54 price means revenue depends entirely on conversion, and there is no sales figure yet.
- The release date was confused (Steam said 9 November, the developer said 10 November), and the demo and full game disagreed on lobby size.
- Asymmetric multiplayer games are prone to balance complaints and empty lobbies once the launch crowd leaves.

## 6. Potato Please

**Genre:** physics-based potato-peeling sim with incremental upgrades and automation; black comedy in a Soviet-style setting
**Platform:** PC (Steam)
**Team:** Golden Horn Studios, self-published (size not published)
**Price:** $9.99 ($8.99 at launch)
**Sales:** none announced. About 1,900 reviews at 88% positive within a week of its 3 November launch. Using the common rule of thumb of 30–50 sales per review, that suggests somewhere around 60–95k copies. That range is my estimate, not a reported figure.
**Critic score:** 7.5/10 from Inven Global.

### Why it succeeded

- **It was built on an existing meme.** The developer says the idea came from a potato meme already circulating online, so the audience existed before the game did.
- **The title borrows recognition** from *Papers, Please*, and the setting backs it up: quotas for troops at the front, letters from a son, secret notes hidden inside potatoes, moral choices.
- **Short and complete.** About five hours to the first ending, with a deliberate arc from peeling by hand with a razor blade to full automation.
- **Tactile core action.** Rotating and peeling with physics is satisfying to do and to watch.

### Marketing breakdown

- The trailer circulated on 20 September; launch followed six weeks later.
- **The plagiarism dispute generated coverage.** Polish developer Radosław Klasa, who announced a potato-peeling game called *1,000,000 Potatoes* in July, accused Potato Please of being an AI-generated clone. Golden Horn replied the next day that it had never seen his game. The dispute is unresolved, and the reviewer who covered it found the game "far from" low-effort AI output.
- **Luck or strategy:** meme timing was strategy; the controversy was not planned but raised awareness.

### What a solo developer can replicate

- Watch for memes with a physical, repeatable action in them and build a small, complete game around one, fast.
- A five-hour game at $10 is a realistic solo scope.
- Give a joke premise a real ending. The emptiness after automation is the point of the story here.

### Red flags

- **The clone accusation could have sunk the launch** and is still attached to the game's name in search results.
- Frequent physics bugs (floating potatoes, erratic carts).
- English text shows signs of machine translation.
- The controls suit a controller better than mouse and keyboard, on a PC-only release.
- If the other developer's game ships, the two will be compared directly.

## 7. Magical Girl ER (こちら魔法少女救命外科)

**Genre:** horror medical triage sim, single-player
**Platform:** PC (Steam)
**Team:** noranuco, solo developer, self-published
**Price:** ¥770 (about $5), 10% launch discount
**Sales:** 10,000+ units in about two weeks (developer, via Steam community post)
**Gross ceiling:** roughly $50k.
**Reviews:** Very Positive.

This is the smallest result in the report and the most relevant one for a solo developer.

### Why it succeeded

- **A two-genre collision that sells itself in a sentence:** you triage magical girls injured in kaiju attacks. It takes a beloved anime genre and looks at the part the anime never shows.
- **Systems, not content.** You have limited beds and limited stamina, patients have hidden conditions, and you diagnose from tools, dialogue and documents. Then you choose who to admit and who to turn away.
- **Replay through branching.** Eleven endings over a one-week in-game shift, so a short game supports multiple runs and discussion.
- **Horror through implication.** The store page warns of blood and jump scares, but the game avoids extreme gore, which keeps it streamable.
- **Price.** At about $5 it is an impulse buy.

### Marketing breakdown

- **Japanese games press picked it up before and at launch:** Automaton (in English and Japanese), Denfaminicogamer, GameWith. Automaton then ran a second story on the 10k milestone.
- The developer is active on X and announced the milestone in a Steam community post.
- No evidence of paid marketing, a publisher or a Next Fest push in the sources I found.
- **Luck or strategy:** the hook was the strategy. A premise this clear writes its own headline, and niche press covers it for free.

### What a solo developer can replicate

- Nearly all of it. One person, one location, a week-long structure, 2D art, a low price.
- Write the premise as a headline first. If a journalist can paste it as the title of an article, it is strong enough.
- Email niche and regional press directly; they covered a ¥770 solo game twice.
- Post the milestone publicly. "10k sold" produced a second round of coverage.
- Tell players what comes next. The developer is openly weighing a large update or a sequel.

### Red flags

- **$50k gross is not a living** after Steam's cut and tax. The value here is the audience and the sequel option.
- The developer disclosed generative AI use for some localisation and store text, which is a reputational risk with this audience.
- The audience is concentrated in Japan; reach outside it depends on translation quality.

## Also worth noting

- **Abyssal Lapidary** (gothic gem-cutting sim, unreleased): grew wishlists 427% during Next Fest, from about 28k to 149,800, after releasing its demo just before the fest. A strange, specific craft premise outperformed nearly every big-name demo.
- **Find The Needle** (incremental, unreleased): third most-played Next Fest demo.
- **ZED ZONE** (solo developer Leven Liu, published by INDIECN): reached 1.0 on 5 November after three years in Early Access, adding co-op and a new island at 40% off. About 4,000 reviews at 82%. A reminder that a 1.0 launch is a second marketing event.
- **Underkeep** (Rake in Grass): 1990s-style dungeon crawler launched 5 November on Steam and GOG, 95% positive on an early sample of about 120 reviews.

## Patterns for a solo developer

### 1. Steam Next Fest no longer creates demand

October had 7,319 demos, against 2,963 a year earlier. The median game gained 60 wishlists in the week. Even a top-10% game gained only about 1,500, half of what it would have in June. GameDiscoverCo's conclusion is that the fest amplifies interest that existed before it started.

**What to do:** release the demo weeks or months before the fest, build an audience first, and use the fest as the final push into launch. Probably Stolen had a playtest in August and a demo in September. Abyssal Lapidary released its demo just before the fest.

### 2. Launch right after the fest

Probably Stolen launched two days after the fest. Worst Class Ever pulled its demo and launched two weeks after. Both converted a warm demo audience.

### 3. The premise has to work as a headline

- "Triage magical girls injured in kaiju attacks."
- "Clean the Moon with a toothbrush."
- "Run a pawn shop where everything is probably stolen."
- "Scam with your friends."

Every game in this report can be described in under ten words, and in most cases the title does it.

### 4. Price follows format

- **Co-op/party games: $5–$10**, with a launch discount. They sell in groups, and the price has to clear the least interested friend.
- **Single-player games with 20+ hours: $20**, with a 10–15% launch discount.
- **Short single-player games: $5–$10.**

### 5. Positioning can be the whole problem

Deepspace Emporium and Probably Stolen are the same core game. One peaked at 28 players and the other at over 150,000. Before abandoning a game that tests badly, check whether the few people who play it keep playing. If they do, change the name, capsule and art before changing the design.

### 6. Generative AI is now a launch risk

Four of the seven games drew AI accusations or disclosures: Moon Sweepers (reviews dispute the disclosure), Potato Please (clone accusation), Scam With Your Friends (running costs), Magical Girl ER (disclosed for localisation). PC Gamer counted AI disclosures among the top Next Fest demos. If you use it, disclose precisely, and expect it to be raised in reviews either way.

### 7. Realistic expectations

The six-figure results here all had either a publisher, a multiplayer viral loop, or both. The one clearly solo, self-published, single-player game sold 10,000 copies at $5. That is a good result and it is the honest baseline for a first solo release with a strong hook.
